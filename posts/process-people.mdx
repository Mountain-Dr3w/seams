I've sat in a lot of meetings where we checked that every review, handoff, and sign-off had happened, but barely discussed whether the work was any good. Keeping track of those steps matters in a large organization. Things fall through cracks, and someone needs to notice. I've also watched teams keep following a process long after anyone could explain what it was helping them decide.

It gets difficult when someone's professional identity is wrapped up in that process. A designer might fight to keep a research phase because removing it feels like an attack on research as a discipline, even when the proposed study won't change the team's decision. A PM might insist on a prioritization framework everyone has learned to work around, because running it is how they demonstrate that they're doing their job. The framework may be fine. But if everyone leaves with the same priorities they brought in, I want to know why we're spending the time.

I've been thinking about this as AI coding tools change how much of my design process I need for a given project. I noticed the attachment in my own work, and it's been harder to let go of than I expected.

## Translation layers

I wrote in my [first post](https://seams.velveteen.sh/becoming-a-builder) about what happened when I started building software myself. What I didn't fully say there is how many of my own design artifacts stopped being necessary once I could make a rough version and test it.

I'd used those artifacts to help other people build. A journey map could explain where users were struggling. A spec could record the behavior we had agreed on before handing the work to engineering. I got good at service blueprints because they let me influence what got built without needing to build it myself.

When I'm doing the implementation too, I don't always need the same handoff documents. Some still help me think; others duplicate decisions I can work through in the product. I'm also finding that the documents I keep need to be usable by both teammates and the models I work with. That tension between writing for humans and writing for models is probably my next article.

## The blueprint I didn't need

When I started building software directly, I kept reaching for a service blueprint first, even when I could have built a rough version and tested whether it worked. Sometimes I needed to think through the service before touching the code. Other times I was reaching for the part of the job I already knew I was good at.

I'm still catching myself doing this. This week I planned research activities that, when I looked at them again, weren't going to change the team's decision. I had to cancel them before we started. Apparently noticing the habit once wasn't enough.

In several cases, skipping the blueprint and putting a rough version in front of someone taught me more than the blueprint would have. I still felt reluctant to skip it. I'd spent years getting good at that work, and I liked being the person people came to for it. Knowing I could still make a blueprint didn't quite settle the feeling that I ought to be making one.

## Incentive structures

It's easier to change methods when your job is understood as solving a problem. If your job is understood as running a particular process, dropping that process can leave you wondering what you're being paid for. A lot of organizations teach people to think that way.

You get promoted for running a workshop well, and you learn to make the workshop look good. Your performance review asks whether you produced a research report, so you make sure there's a report. Whether either one changed a product decision can be much harder to explain in a review. I can see why people get attached to the visible part of their work.

AI makes this harder to ignore. If a tool can produce a convincing journey map in minutes, the finished map doesn't tell you much about how carefully someone studied the problem. It never told you the whole story, but it was an easy thing to point to. I'd rather be asked what I learned, which decision it changed, and whether that helped the people using the product.

Quick shout-out to my company, Rise8, here. They evaluate the outcomes and impact of the work. It's one of the reasons I love it there. I wish that were more common in the organizations I've worked with.

## What problem do you solve?

The designers I know who are handling these changes well can describe their work without starting with a deliverable. They talk about figuring out why users abandon a workflow, or fixing something people struggle with every day. Some have picked up Claude Code and started building directly. Others are spending more time with users or working more closely with engineers. They have something specific they're trying to improve, which helps them decide whether a method is still useful.

I find that harder than having a defined design process. A process gives me a place to start and a way to know I've done the work. Without it, I have to decide whether a blueprint will help, whether I need more research, or whether I should build something and see what happens. I don't always get that decision right. Canceling those research activities this week was uncomfortable, but running them just because I'd planned them would have wasted the team's time.

In [Becoming a Builder](https://seams.velveteen.sh/becoming-a-builder), I wrote about having a conversation with your material, an idea from woodworking and ceramics. That's helped me understand my attachment to process. For years, a blueprint was one of the few ways I could affect what got built. Now I can also change the software and watch someone use it. I still make blueprints when I need them. I'm trying to get better at knowing when I don't.
