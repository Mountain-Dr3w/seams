I've been a designer for nearly a decade now. Product design, service design, roles where you spend weeks mapping out how something should behave and then write it up in a way that helps someone else go build it. I loved it. I loved the systems thinking, and I loved being the person in the room who could talk to users and come back with the thing nobody had named yet. That part of the job still matters to me.

After a while, though, I started noticing how many ideas would just sit there. You put together a journey map, a service blueprint, or a prototype and it goes into a backlog. Sometimes it gets built. Sometimes it gets half-built, or built later in a way that doesn't match what you intended, or it never moves because the engineering team had other priorities. I had Figma files and Miro boards and decks that people nodded at in meetings and then forgot about. I don't think I understood how much that was wearing on me until I started being able to do something about it.

## picking up the tools

AI coding tools got good enough that I could start building applications with logins, databases, and deployment pipelines. I understood how I wanted software to behave, but I'd rarely written production code. Suddenly I could get further than a prototype.

I described features in plain language, and the AI generated code. A lot of that code was broken or weird or structured in ways I didn't understand. Ask me about the onboarding portal I vibe coded. Better yet, ask the engineers on my team who had to fix it. I iterated, asked questions, and slowly started recognizing patterns. I was getting a feel for code I could follow and change, even when I couldn't write it from scratch. I spent months feeling slightly lost inside my own project. It was also the most fun I've had at a computer in years.

Building made me deal with things I'd been able to leave out of a design. A clean onboarding flow looks different when an API call takes four seconds instead of one and you have to decide what the user sees while they wait. That feedback had always been available, but I was a level removed from it. Watching my own build fall over made it harder to put off.

There's an idea in woodworking and ceramics about having a conversation with your material. A woodworker has to respond to the grain, even when it doesn't suit the plan. I kept thinking about that while building. I'd spent years describing how software should work without spending much time working in the code itself. I was finding out what I'd missed.

## where the field is going

I have opinions about where the design field is headed. They're convenient opinions for someone in my position, but I think they're right.

I first entered the design field as part of the Air Force's Kessel Run. Since then, the career path I've seen has tended toward more abstraction. You start out making things and you "grow" into making strategy decks about things. More frameworks, more workshops, more operating models. Seniority often means spending less time making the product and more time organizing how it gets made. I understood the reasoning: if engineering time was scarce, helping a team decide what to build was a useful place to spend mine.

I can now build and test things that I used to need an engineering team to take on. That changes what I expect of my own design work, and I suspect it will change what teams expect of designers too. Complex systems still need people who can think across the whole service. Being able to build a working version gives those people another way to test whether their thinking holds up.

For a long time, I was anxious about being the person who thought through a thing and then waited for someone else to make it. I was good at that job, which made it hard to try being bad at a different one. The designers I talk to who are excited about their work right now are building alongside it: shipping internal tools, prototyping in code, standing up small apps for their teams. I recognize that excitement.

I **WANT** the field to go in this direction. I'm not a neutral observer. I want to take something from idea to live product myself. The deeper I get into building, the more respect I have for engineering as a discipline. The best design work I've done has been the stuff I also helped build.

A quick note on titles: I don't know whether "builder" will stick. I've been an ISSO, a product designer, a service designer, and a PM. Those were different jobs, but I kept coming back to the same questions about what was broken for the people using a service and what I could do to help. I'm less interested in settling on another title than in getting better at this part of the work.

## velveteen

The name Velveteen comes from The Velveteen Rabbit, a children's book about a toy that becomes real through being loved. I have a one-year-old daughter who loves books, so the name came naturally. The apps people are building with AI tools remind me of that story. Someone cared enough about a problem to describe a solution and keep working on it until it ran on their laptop. Getting it online so someone else can use it is where a lot of us get stuck.

I kept hitting that wall myself. I'd build something I was proud of and then spend days fighting with Docker, environment variables, and deployment pipelines. I wanted to work on the app, but first I had to learn how to run it safely outside my laptop. I started noticing the same difficulty among people building from experience in their own fields: teachers making classroom tools, nonprofit workers automating work that ate up their week. They knew what they needed the software to do. Deployment was a separate problem to learn.

Velveteen is what I'm building to help with that. You connect your GitHub, pick a repo, and it figures out your framework, generates the infrastructure files you're missing, runs a security pipeline, and explains anything it finds in language you can act on. When everything passes, it deploys to a live URL. I wrote more about the specifics on the [about page](https://velveteen.sh/about), and the full technical breakdown is [under the hood](https://velveteen.sh/about/under-the-hood).

I work on this before my day job starts. I keep thinking about the small, specific apps people could put to use if getting them online took less work. I'd like Velveteen to make that part easier than it was for me.

## what comes next

I'll use this blog to write about building Velveteen: the technical and design decisions, what I got wrong, and what I had to redo. I'm still learning how much I don't know about shipping software. Writing about it as I go seems more useful than trying to reconstruct it later, once I've forgotten which parts were hard.
